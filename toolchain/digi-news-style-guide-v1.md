# DIGI NEWS — Headline & Style Guide (v1)

The rubric decides what is true enough to print; this guide decides how it reads. It is binding on headlines, standfirsts and the first two paragraphs, and advisory everywhere else.

All the examples below are real Digi News headlines and paragraphs, with rewrites. Each rewrite was checked against the piece's own figures and caveat.

---

## 1. Headlines

**The rules**

- **One idea.** If the headline needs "but", "after", "while" or a full stop to hold two facts, pick one. The second fact goes in the standfirst.
- **About 12 words or fewer.** The median Digi News headline is 14 words, and four in ten run longer. Front-page cards and phone screens truncate them.
- **Lead with the subject and an active verb.** "UK smoking rate halves", not "Fewer than one in ten UK adults now smoke".
- **Rest on the claim that survives the caveat (rubric §5b).** The headline is written last, after the caveat card.
- **No attribution tail unless it is needed for accuracy.** "…, NOAA data show" adds four words. The standfirst names the source.
- **Numbers.** Use at most one figure in a headline, and only if it is the story.

**Before and after**

| Original | Problem | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| UK growth was second-fastest in the G7 this spring, after a 2025 in which two quarters recorded none (18 words) | Two ideas. The piece's own caveat says the G7 ranking is within revision noise. | **Revisions show UK economy stalled twice in 2025 before spring pickup** |
| Fewer than one in ten UK adults now smoke, but the rate is five times higher without qualifications (18) | Two ideas. The caveat says "fewer than one in ten" may straddle the line, while the halving is robust. | **UK adult smoking rate has halved since 2011** — the qualifications gap leads the standfirst |
| The world's oceans have set a temperature record four months running, NOAA data show (14) | Attribution tail. It also ran the day after a near-identical piece. | **Sea surface temperatures set a record for a fourth month running** — and run it as a follow-up to the 9 October piece, or not at all |
| NOAA changed how it measures El Niño — at this stage, that moves 2026 from first place to fourth (18) | The reader must hold the method change and the ranking at once. | **On NOAA's new index, this El Niño ranks fourth for its stage, not first** |
| UK borrowing is below last year's pace but £8.1bn above forecast, with benefits the biggest spending rise (17) | Three ideas. | **UK borrowing runs £8.1bn above OBR forecast as benefit spending rises** |
| In four months, Bundibugyo Ebola has recorded more than twice the cases of DR Congo's largest previous outbreak (18) | Throat-clearing opener. | **DR Congo's Ebola outbreak is now twice the size of its largest before** |

**Keep doing this:** *Bookmakers price Count Binface near 10/1 in Clacton. Polymarket prices him at 1%.* It sets up two short, concrete, parallel facts with the tension built in. The two sentences are one idea: the gap.

## 2. Standfirsts

The standfirst carries what the headline dropped. It should be two sentences, and no more than about 40 words.

- **Sentence one** gives the key measured figure, with its source and date.
- **Sentence two** gives the most important qualification or second finding.

Do not end on a hedge the headline did not need.

**Before** (Aviva, 25 July):
> Between its 5 June low and 24 July the insurer returned 14.6% against a FTSE 100 up 3.6%. Over the five weeks in which its sector index can be measured alongside it, Aviva lagged that index. The gap is a benchmark artefact, not company news.

**Problem.** The final sentence claims more than the piece shows. The body says that half the rise falls before the sector comparison begins, and that the desk "cannot separate" a sector explanation from a company one for that half. The window also starts at a low the desk chose. The standfirst should not hide either point.

**After:**
> Measured from its June low, the insurer rose 14.6% to 24 July, against 3.6% for the FTSE 100. For the five weeks that can be compared with its sector index, Aviva moved in line with other life insurers; the first half of the rise has no sector comparison.

## 3. The first two paragraphs

The reader decides in the first two paragraphs whether to continue. Those paragraphs answer three questions, in this order:

1. What happened?
2. Why does it matter, or why now?
3. How sure are we?

The detail comes after.

**Number ration.** No sentence in the first two paragraphs carries more than two figures. The smoking, GDP and asylum pieces of 11–12 October carry one figure every six to eight words of prose, which is denser than a statistical bulletin. Put exact figures the argument does not need into an exhibit or its data table. In prose, round where precision adds nothing: write "about half" rather than "48.5% of its 2011 level".

**Before** (smoking, 12 October — nine figures in four sentences):
> The Office for National Statistics (ONS) estimates that 9.8% of UK adults aged 18 and over smoked in 2025, about 4.9 million people. In 2011 the figure was 20.2%, so prevalence fell by 10.4 percentage points and is now 48.5% of its 2011 level. Men (11.3%) were more likely to smoke than women (8.2%). By nation, the rate was 9.5% in England, 9.9% in Northern Ireland, 11.1% in Wales and 11.4% in Scotland.

**After:**
> About 4.9 million adults in the UK smoked last year, according to the Office for National Statistics — 9.8% of over-18s, half the rate of 2011.[1] Men still smoke more than women, and Scotland and Wales more than England.[1] Those gaps are small next to the one between people with and without qualifications.

The nation and sex figures move to a small table or the exhibit.

## 4. Why now

Every piece says, early on, what prompted it. This is usually one clause.

> "Figures published by the Home Office in late August show…"

If the release is old, say why the piece is running now:

> "Ahead of the next release in [month], the August figures show a shift that has had little attention: …"

A piece that cannot answer "why now?" in a clause has failed rubric §0.

---

## 5. Register

- **Plain verbs.** Use "said", not "stated" or "noted". Use "rose" and "fell", not "saw an increase". Use "about", not "approximately".
- **Write as a news desk, not about one.** Avoid "this desk takes no view", "this desk reports that reading rather than endorsing it" and "worth being precise about". Say the thing: "No study isolates the effect of gilt yields on insurers' shares over this period."
- **No boilerplate signposting.** Phrases like "It is worth noting", "Importantly" and "The arithmetic is straightforward" can be cut every time.
- **One qualification per claim.** If a sentence needs three hedges, the claim is too strong; weaken the claim instead.
- **Name people and bodies in full on first use** (Office for National Statistics), then use the short form (ONS).
- **Dates.** Write "12 October", not "12 Oct", in prose. Use "last year" and "this spring" only when the dateline makes them unambiguous.

---

## 6. Links and follow-ups

- When an earlier Digi News piece covered the topic, link to it in the sentence that mentions the earlier finding: "Digi News [reported in August](…) that NOAA's switch to a relative index moved 2026 down the rankings."
- A follow-up's headline says what is new. It does not restate the earlier headline with an updated number.
