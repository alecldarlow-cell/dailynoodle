# NOODLE NEWS — Standards & Methodology Rubric (v5)

The binding reference for every Noodle News piece. When a judgement call arises, this document governs.

v5 keeps every v4 standard and its section numbers, because the linter cites them (§1a, §3, §8, §10, §12, §13). It moves the desk from "what does the release say" to "what happened, why, and what it means": a so-what test (§0), a fixed news spine (§0a), a context floor (§1d), a search for explanations before declaring causes unknown (§4b), a cap on the caveat block (§6), a "what happens next" slot (§6b) and Medium as the default length (§9). The change log is at the end.

---

## 0. What earns a piece *(new in v4)*

A piece is commissioned because it tells a reader something they did not know and could not easily get elsewhere. Accuracy is necessary, but it is not sufficient. Before research starts, the piece must pass three tests.

- **The peg.** Why now? Name the release, event or decision the piece hangs on, and its date. If the source was published more than 14 days before the piece, either find a fresher peg or say in the first paragraph why the piece is running now.
- **Added value.** Write one sentence the piece will say that the source's own headline does not. A rewrite of a statistical bulletin in the bulletin's order is not a Noodle News piece. Added value can come from:
  - a longer time series;
  - a comparison the source does not draw;
  - a breakdown buried in the tables;
  - a check of an official claim against the data;
  - or a correction of a common misreading.
- **The so-what *(new in v5)*.** Write one sentence naming the real-world consequence: for a person, a public service, a budget, a market, or a decision that is pending. "The figure rose" is not a consequence; "the NHS met its target and then stopped improving, with the 2029 promise no closer" is. No sentence, no commission. Added value from data alone (below) is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Not already run.** Search the feed for the topic over the last 30 days. If Noodle News has covered it, the new piece is a follow-up. It links to the earlier piece and leads on what has changed. If nothing material has changed, do not run it.

A piece that fails any test is reframed or dropped at the spine checkpoint, not after the build.

### 0a. The news spine *(new in v5)*

Every Medium or Long news piece is organised around four questions, in this order. Each gets its own section (the eyebrow may be worded freely, but must make the job clear):

1. **What happened.** The news, in plain words, with the measured figure.
2. **Why it happened.** The explanations on the record, attributed and weighed (§4b). If credible sources disagree, this is where the case and counter-case go.
3. **Why it matters / who it affects.** The consequence named in the so-what sentence: people, services, money, the decision it feeds.
4. **What happens next.** The next decision, deadline, release or test, with its date where one exists (§6b).

The data is the evidence for this story, not the story. Exhibits sit inside the section they support. An evergreen explainer may use *context → causes → comparison → consequences* instead, but must still cover why and so-what.

Statistics are the desk's strength and stay its spine of evidence. The aim is the standard of the Economist, the FT and the Guardian: each answers "so what?" by the third paragraph and organises everything after around it. Noodle News does not do direct reporting; it reads, checks and attributes what is already on the record.

## 1. Source hierarchy

Use the highest tier available for each claim. A claim is only as strong as its weakest load-bearing source.

- **Tier 1 — Primary and official.**
  - Peer-reviewed research.
  - National statistics agencies (ONS, Eurostat, BLS, etc.).
  - Central banks and regulators.
  - Official records, registers and legislation.
  - Court judgments.
  - Audited accounts.
  - Original datasets.
- **Tier 2 — Established institutions.**
  - Reputable research institutes, and think tanks with transparent methods.
  - International bodies (IMF, OECD, WHO, UN).
  - Professional bodies.
  - Libraries and archives (e.g. House of Commons Library).
- **Tier 3 — Quality press, for events and attribution.** Established news organisations with corrections policies (wire services such as PA and Reuters, the BBC, FT, Guardian, Times, Telegraph, Economist and their equivalents). Use them for *what happened, who said what, and which explanations named people have offered*, always attributed. Not for analysis presented as fact. *(v5: the reported positions and explanations of named people are an expected use of Tier 3, not a fallback.)*
- **Do not use as load-bearing sources:**
  - opinion or comment columns;
  - advocacy or single-issue campaign sites;
  - anonymous blogs and social posts;
  - content farms;
  - outlets without editorial accountability.

  These may be *named and attributed* ("X argued that…"), never cited as established fact.

**Verification.** Any surprising, pivotal or counter-intuitive figure must be corroborated by a second independent Tier 1/2 source. Where sources conflict, present the range and name the disagreement rather than picking silently.

**Independence floor *(new in v4)*.** Every piece cites at least one source independent of its primary release. That source corroborates, gives context or tests the release's framing. Two editions of the same release, or the same body's press notice, do not count. If no independent source exists, the Method says so, and the piece is short tier.

### 1a. Citation traceability

Every load-bearing claim carries an **inline numbered citation** at the point of claim. Each one resolves to a numbered entry in the footer Sources block.

Each source entry is tagged with its **tier badge — T1 / T2 / T3** — so evidentiary strength is visible without leaving the sentence. Grouped, un-numbered source lists are not sufficient.

A claim whose only support is a Tier 3 outlet must be written as attribution ("the *FT* reports…"), not as bare fact.

### 1b. Go to the data, not the bulletin *(new in v4)*

A statistical bulletin is a summary written by the producer, and it selects what to show. The underlying tables usually hold the series the bulletin leaves out. These include the earlier years, the breakdowns and the confidence intervals.

- When the bulletin text lacks a figure the piece needs, look for it in the release's datasets or reference tables before writing around the gap.
- If the dataset cannot be reached from the sandbox, ask the requester to attach it. State this at the spine checkpoint.
- "What we don't know" is for things that are **not known**. Things that are published but were not retrieved do not belong there. If a published figure was not retrieved, say so plainly: "the ONS publishes this by year in its reference tables; this piece did not retrieve them". Never present it as a gap in the evidence.

### 1c. Published positions of named organisations *(new in v4)*

Noodle News does not conduct interviews or send requests for comment. It does report what named people and organisations have **already said on the record**. This covers:

- official statements;
- press releases;
- ministerial or parliamentary answers;
- company announcements;
- on-record comments carried by Tier 3 outlets.

- Where a piece reaches a critical or adverse finding about a named organisation or person, look for their published position on that finding and report it.
  - If one exists, include it.
  - If none can be found, say "[organisation] has not published a response to this" rather than staying silent.
- Quotes follow §11: under 15 words, one per source, and attributed with the date and venue.

### 1d. Context floor *(new in v5)*

The primary release tells the reader what changed. The context sources tell them why it matters. Every Medium or Long news piece cites, in addition to its primary release:

- at least one **Tier 2 analysis** of the topic (for example IFS, Resolution Foundation, Health Foundation, King's Fund, Nuffield Trust, Institute for Government, Migration Observatory, OBR, Bank of England commentary, a learned society); and
- where a **policy, target, promise or pending decision** is involved: the responsible body's stated position and the strongest on-record critic or independent assessment, reported under §1c and §11.

A typical Medium piece therefore carries four to eight sources. A piece built on one release and nothing else is a brief (§9), not a news piece, and says so.

## 2. Numbers discipline

- Compute every derived statistic **in code**; never estimate by eye. Re-run and re-check before publishing. The printed figure must equal the computed figure; a common failure is a hand-rounded number drifting from the data.
- Re-check totals, rates and growth figures. Watch for errors of unit, base year, nominal versus real, and per-capita basis.
- State units and the as-of date. Use tabular figures in exhibits.
- Label projections, assumptions and scenarios explicitly. Show the counterfactual where one materially changes the reading.
- Never truncate or invert a chart axis to exaggerate. If a non-zero baseline is used, say so in the caption. The chart toolkit defaults to honest, zero-baselined value axes.

### 2a. Choose measures that don't mislead

Interrogate the obvious metric before using it. Check for:

- floor or ceiling effects;
- base-rate effects;
- small-denominator noise;
- window or edge artifacts.

Pick the clearest honest measure, explain in plain terms how it was built, and show the assumptions.

**Windows *(new in v4)*.** If the desk chooses a start or end date, say so in the body, not only in the Method. Examples are a share price "from its low" or a series "since the peak". Test the finding from other reasonable dates. A finding that exists only because of the window chosen is not a finding.

## 3. The assumption firewall *(P0)*

Projections, scenarios and modelled "what-ifs" are useful, but they must be quarantined from anything that carries authority.

- **Never load-bearing for the headline, standfirst or any hero stat.** Hero figures and the headline state *measured* facts only. Lead with the recorded figure and introduce the scenario afterwards.
  - A conditional ("if X happens, Y will be…") is a scenario, and may not be a hero stat.
- **Structurally separate in the data.** A projected series, segment or value is a distinct field (see `DN-API.md` for the field name). It is never silently spliced into measured data.
- **Visually unmistakable.**
  - Projections render dashed (lines) or hatched (spans and areas).
  - Scenario ranges render as a shaded band.
  - The caption names it a scenario and states its basis.
- A projection may *support* a narrative ("on current trends, X could reach…"). The reader must never have to guess where measurement ends and assumption begins.

## 4. Fact vs interpretation

Keep them visibly separate. Verifiable facts read plainly and carry a citation. Analysis is introduced as analysis ("This suggests…", "One reading is…"). Never let interpretation borrow the authority of a cited fact.

### 4a. Attribution discipline

Causal and evaluative claims must be **attributed or marked as analysis**, never asserted as near-fact. Phrases like "most observers point to…" are not permitted as standalone fact, and nor is an unsourced list of "causes". Either:

- cite who finds the cause, and on what evidence; or
- frame it explicitly as the desk's reading and note its uncertainty.

### 4b. Search for the explanation before calling it unknown *(new in v5)*

Data releases rarely explain themselves; the people around them usually do. Before writing that the figures "cannot say why", "say nothing about why", or that a cause is unknown, search for the explanations already on the record:

- the producer's own commentary and the responsible department or regulator;
- targets, deadlines, policy changes, funding decisions or events that coincide with the change;
- Tier 2 analysis and Tier 3 reporting of the same release.

Report what you find, attributed, and weigh it: which explanation the evidence supports, which it does not, and what is still open. "What we don't know" then holds only what remains unexplained after that search. Declaring a cause unknown when a named body has publicly explained it is an error of omission and is treated like a factual error.

## 5. Contested-topic protocol

Apply this **only when a question is genuinely disputed among credible sources**. Do not manufacture false balance on settled matters.

- Give the **strongest, good-faith version** of each major position (steelman, not strawman), each with its own sourcing.
- Split **empirical disputes** (what is true — evidence can sometimes resolve these) from **value disputes** (what we should prefer — evidence cannot). Label which kind each card is.
- **Verdict rule.** State an empirical bottom line only where the weight of reliable evidence clearly points. On value questions, lay out the positions and stop. Always note the strongest objection to whatever conclusion is drawn.

### 5a. Headline-overclaim guard

The headline and standfirst may not claim more certainty than the evidence supports, and may not rest on a projection (see §3).

A "record", "first" or "highest ever" in a headline must be true of the *measured* data as printed. It may not be true only once a scenario is assumed. If the striking claim depends on an assumption, the headline states the measured fact and the body explains the scenario.

### 5b. The caveat test *(new in v4)*

Write the caveat card **before** finalising the headline and standfirst. Then check whether the strongest objection lands on a specific claim the headline or standfirst makes. Typical targets are:

- a ranking;
- a threshold ("fewer than one in ten");
- a "record";
- a causal verb;
- an exclusion ("not company news").

If it does, **rewrite the headline or standfirst** so that it rests on the claim that survives the objection.

The caveat card is a test of the headline. It is not a place to concede that the headline is shaky. If the objection survives the rewrite, it stays on the card. If it no longer touches the headline, the card states the next-strongest objection.

---

## 6. Uncertainty disclosure

Say plainly when data is thin, lagged or modelled, or when causation is unestablished (e.g. "this is a correlation; no study yet isolates cause").

Every piece carries a short **"What we don't know"** note unless there is genuinely nothing material to disclose. A caveat card states the single strongest objection to the main reading (see §5b). False confidence is the failure mode to avoid.

**Proportion *(new in v5)*.** Honesty is a box, not a quarter of the piece. Together, "What we don't know" and the caveat card take no more than about 15% of the narrative (roughly 150–200 words in a Medium piece). Each says its point once. Measurement mechanics (rounding, revisions, provisional status, which table a figure came from) go in the Method unless they change the reading. Uncertainty that matters to the main claim belongs in the body where the claim is made, not only in the box.

### 6b. What happens next *(new in v5)*

Every news piece ends its narrative with a short section saying what comes next: the decision the evidence feeds, the deadline or target, the next release, the consultation closing, the trial follow-up, the court date. Give the date when one is public. If nothing is scheduled, say what would change the picture and when it could next be measured. This is reporting of what is on the record; it is never a forecast of the outcome.

"What we don't know" lists genuine unknowns, not unretrieved data (see §1b).

### 6a. Survey, poll and sample reporting

Whenever a survey or poll is cited, state:

- the sample size;
- the margin of error (or credible interval);
- the fielding dates;
- the polling organisation.

Do not report a lead or change that is within the margin of error as if it were real movement. Flag online opt-in panels as such.

## 7. Imagery standards (per type)

Imagery is allowed where it genuinely aids understanding. Each type has its own bar.

- **Charts and data exhibits.** Real, sourced data only, with honest axes, labelled units and the source in the caption. This is the default and preferred visual. Build with the house chart toolkit (see §10), so axes, palette and accessibility are consistent.
- **Maps and diagrams.** Built from data or official boundaries, with the source cited. No decorative distortion.
- **Photographs.** Only clearly licensed, public-domain, or wire/official images with a visible credit.
  - Never reproduce copyrighted images.
  - Never misrepresent the event.
  - If no clean image exists, fall back to a typographic or data treatment.
- **AI-generated visuals.** Only as clearly **labelled illustration**. Never as evidence, and never depicting real people or events as if real.
- **In all cases:** no image that could mislead about scale, time, place or identity.

## 8. Accessibility floor

Every piece must clear this bar.

- Each chart has an `aria-label`. The toolkit also emits an SVG `<title>`/`<desc>` and a visually hidden data **table** mirroring the chart, so the figures are machine-readable and auditable.
- Colour is never the sole carrier of meaning; pair it with labels, position or a legend. The categorical palette is colourblind-safe (Okabe-Ito), and the subject accent (cobalt) is reserved for the subject.
- Charts are legible on a phone. The toolkit renders at **1 SVG unit = 1 CSS pixel** and re-renders on resize, so label type does not shrink below ~11px on small screens. Do not ship a chart whose labels are sub-legible at 360px wide.
- **No label may clip off the canvas or overlap another label.** The toolkit measures every structural label before drawing it:
  - left gutters are sized to the widest label;
  - over-long category and row labels wrap, then ellipsise, with the full text kept in the data table and an SVG `<title>`;
  - axis ticks thin when they would collide;
  - edge ticks are clamped on-canvas.

  **Label budgets differ by primitive and by width.** They are measured and tabulated in `DN-API.md`, which is the operative reference. *(v4: replaces the v3 rule of thumb of "~22 characters", which was wrong in both directions.)*

  Free annotations (`notes`, reference-line labels, brackets) are placed by the author and are *not* auto-fitted beyond an anti-clip clamp. The geometry gate flags any that still overlap, and the author resolves them. Verify with `dn-probe.js` at 360px and 680px before shipping.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` is respected; text keeps adequate contrast; layout is responsive in portrait.

## 9. Length tiers

These are targets, not hard limits; the story sets the final length.

- **Short (brief).** ~400–700 words. One hero exhibit, optionally with one supporting exhibit. A single clear point. *(v5: no longer the default. Use only for a genuinely self-contained update, such as a monthly figure with nothing new to explain, and set `dn:read` accordingly.)*
- **Medium (default, v5).** ~900–1,300 words. 2–3 exhibits. The full news spine of §0a: *what happened → why → why it matters → what happens next*.
- **Long.** ~1,800–2,500 words. 4+ exhibits. Full treatment: *context → causes → comparison → consequences → reference*. It includes a reference appendix table and a **"Go deeper"** tail listing what was deliberately left out.

Include the "Go deeper / Read more" tail only when material was consciously omitted to hold length.

**`dn:read` *(new in v4)*** takes one form only: `~N min`. N is the narrative word count divided by 230, rounded, with a minimum of 2. The linter infers the tier from N:

- ≤4 is short;
- 5–7 is medium;
- ≥8 is long.

So the reading time and the tier must agree. Forms such as "medium", "6 min" and "3 min read" are not used.

## 10. The chart toolkit

Exhibits are built with the embedded **DN** library in the house template, as self-contained SVG with no external dependencies. The primitives, fields and failure modes are documented in `DN-API.md`.

The library bakes in:

- honest scales;
- the projection and scenario rendering of §3;
- the legend rule of §8;
- the accessible data table of §8.

Build bespoke exhibits *with* these helpers rather than reinventing chart code, so every piece inherits the same standards. The goal is one **signature exhibit** per piece, built for the specific story.

## 11. Copyright limits

- Paraphrase by default.
- Any direct quote is under 15 words, and there is at most one per source.
- Never reproduce song lyrics, poems or substantial passages, and never reproduce copyrighted images.
- Summarise in original wording; do not mirror a source's structure or phrasing.

## 12. Standing footer

Every piece ends with these blocks.

- **Method** — how key measures were built, the windows, the assumptions and any projection basis.
- **Sources** — numbered and grouped by what they support, **highest tier first**. Each carries its **T1/T2/T3 badge** and, where possible, a link and retrieval date. Inline citations resolve here.
- **Production note** *(new in v4)* — one standing line, identical on every piece:

  > *Researched, written and checked by an AI system working to the Noodle News standards; edited and published by the Noodle News editor.*

  `dn-build.py` generates it as a "Production" line on every report. Do not author it in `footer.html`; if it is already there, the builder skips its own copy rather than duplicating it.
- **Declaration of interest** *(new in v4, when it applies — see §14)*.
- **Corrections** — "None" until one is needed. Material corrections are logged with a date (see §15).
- **Version and as-of** — a version line and **Figures to [date]**.

## 13. Publication & feed

The operative rules are in the project instructions (§Publication) and are enforced by the linter. They cover:

- the filename;
- the `dn:*` meta block;
- the controlled topic vocabulary;
- the three-tile hero grid;
- the first-stat character limit;
- `dn:statlabel`.

**Topic discipline *(new in v4)*.** Tag by what the piece is *about*, not by who acts in it. Public-finance and borrowing figures are `Economy`, even when the Chancellor is named. Use `UK politics` for elections, parties, Parliament and government conduct. Adding a label to the vocabulary is a deliberate decision recorded here, not a one-off at build time.

---

## 14. Disclosure and interests *(new in v4)*

- **AI production.** Every piece carries the standing production note (§12). The byline reads **"Noodle News data desk"** and is not changed to imply human staff.
- **Declaration of interest.** When a piece covers an organisation in which the editor has a material interest, the piece carries a one-line declaration directly under the standfirst and repeats it in the footer. Material interests are:
  - the editor's employer;
  - a charity of which he is a trustee;
  - a company in which he holds more than a nominal stake.

  The wording is: *"Declaration: the editor of Noodle News works for / is a trustee of / holds shares in [organisation]. The analysis was produced to the same standards as every piece, and the editor did not alter its findings."*

  Only the clauses that apply are used.
- Where a declaration would apply, prefer a sector-level framing over a single-company one, unless the company itself is the news.

## 15. Continuity: follow-ups, links and revisions *(new in v4)*

- **Link prior coverage.** If Noodle News has covered the topic before, the piece links to the earlier piece in the body, at the point the earlier finding is mentioned. A follow-up leads with what has changed since the earlier piece.
- **Supersession.** When a new release overturns an earlier piece's headline finding, add a dated line to the earlier piece's Corrections block, linking forward. Examples are a revised ranking, a record that no longer stands, or a method change.
- **Every version bump is logged.** Any revision after first publication adds a dated, one-line entry to Corrections stating what changed. Use "v1.1 (12 Oct 2026): Exhibit 02 relabelled; no figures changed". A version number that rises with nothing logged is a silent edit, and silent edits are not permitted. Pre-publication rebuilds stay at v1.0. Entries go in `meta.json` as `"corrections": ["v1.1 (12 Oct 2026): …"]`, and `dn-build.py` renders them. It refuses to build a version above v1.0 with no entry.

## 16. Legal and fairness *(new in v4)*

Noodle News is a personal, portfolio-scale publication, but its pieces are public and read like journalism. A light floor applies.

- **No imputation of wrongdoing** to a named person or organisation unless a Tier 1 record establishes it. That means a court finding, a regulator's decision or an official finding.
  - Do not speculate about undisclosed conduct, such as market abuse, stake-building, fraud or misconduct, even to rule it out.
  - If a common explanation implies wrongdoing, say that no public record supports it, and stop.
- **Live legal proceedings.** Report only what is on the public record, and do not comment on the merits or likely outcome.
- **Private individuals** are not named unless they are already prominent in the public record of the story.
- **Market-moving material.** Share-price pieces describe what happened. They do not suggest what a price will do or whether a share is cheap or expensive.
